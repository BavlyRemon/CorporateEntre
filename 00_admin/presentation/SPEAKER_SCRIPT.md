# Speaker Script — "The Vocabulary of Corporate Entrepreneurship"

> **Navigation**: Arrow keys or Space to advance. Each bullet below marks one stage advance.
> Estimated talk time: ~45–60 minutes for Slides 1–7. Appendix is Q&A reference only.

---

## SLIDE 01 — THE HOOK

*[Pause while the title animates in. Let the numbers settle.]*

"Let me start with a simple observation.

CEOs write letters to shareholders every year. These letters are formal, deliberate — every word is reviewed by legal, by comms, by the board. They are not casual. They are performative in the truest sense: the CEO is *performing* the identity of the firm.

So the question this study asks is: what do they say? And more specifically — what does the *vocabulary* they choose reveal about their entrepreneurial strategy?

We looked at 28 letters across four firms, spanning 1997 to 2025. That's 61,318 words of carefully chosen language.

Four companies. Two industries. And the same five analytical lenses applied to all of them.

The thesis is this: the *way* a firm talks about innovation is not just description — it is evidence of the strategic mode the firm is actually operating in."

---

## SLIDE 02 — SCOPE: THE 2×2 DESIGN

"The design is a deliberate 2×2.

Two industries — technology and energy — give us the cross-industry contrast. Within each industry, we picked two firms to test whether the findings survive *within-industry* differences.

On the tech side: **Amazon** and **NVIDIA**. They are both technology companies, but one is B2C — Amazon sells to consumers — and one is B2B — NVIDIA sells to data centres and enterprises. If we find the same pattern in both, it's not a B2C artefact.

On the energy side: **Shell** and **Chevron**. Both are supermajors, but one is a European stakeholder-model firm with a legal seat in London — and one is an American shareholder-primacy firm headquartered in San Ramon. Same industry, different governance cultures.

There's a bonus structural feature on the Amazon side: we capture a *CEO transition*. Bezos wrote letters from 1997 to 2021. Jassy took over in 2022. That gives us a natural experiment — same firm, different leader.

And on the NVIDIA side, we straddle the AI inflection. Letters from 2017 through 2023, then the 2025 letter — written after $115 billion in datacenter revenue. That's a technology discontinuity, and we get to watch what it does to the language."

---

## SLIDE 03 — TECH VS OIL: SIX LENSES

*[This slide has six progressive reveals. Advance once per beat below.]*

"Before we go company by company, let's look at the headline contrast. Six lenses. I'll take them one at a time.

---

**[ADVANCE → Stage 1: Long-term vs Short-term]**

The first lens is long-term vs short-term orientation. And this one is the first surprise — *there is no split*.

All four firms land between 0.84 and 0.90 on long-term share. Amazon, NVIDIA, Shell, Chevron — they all sound the same here.

Long-term language is the wallpaper of CEO letters. Everyone uses it. It is a baseline, not a differentiator. We note it and move on.

---

**[ADVANCE → Stage 2: Explore vs Exploit]**

*Here* is where it breaks open.

Explore-share: Amazon at 0.67, NVIDIA at 0.64. Shell at 0.09. Chevron at 0.25. There's a wide, empty gap in the middle — no firm lands between 0.30 and 0.60.

This is the cleanest industry split in the data. Tech explores. Energy exploits. It is not a matter of degree — it is a categorical difference in strategic vocabulary.

---

**[ADVANCE → Stage 3: Customer vs Shareholder]**

Second clean split — and this one silences an objection.

You might say: of course Amazon is customer-voiced — it's a consumer business. But NVIDIA is B2B, and NVIDIA's customer share is 0.69. Shell is 0.32. Chevron is 0.27.

The split survives the B2B test. It is an *industry* divide, not a business model artefact.

---

**[ADVANCE → Stage 4: The Flip — two themes that reverse direction]**

Now two themes that don't just split — they flip.

The first is Horizon Scanning — the language of sensing the future, naming discontinuity, scanning what's coming next. NVIDIA is at 35 per thousand words. Everyone else is between 7 and 8. NVIDIA scans the horizon at roughly five times the rate of any other firm in this corpus.

The second flip is Strategic Leadership — the vocabulary of governance, ownership, conviction, long-term stewardship. This is the *one* IPM theme where energy out-talks tech. Shell is highest at 2.60 per thousand. Chevron at 1.64. NVIDIA is lowest at 0.67.

The interpretation: fast-moving tech firms don't *narrate* leadership — they let the product strategy carry it. Energy firms, operating in slow physical systems with long capital cycles, have to *perform* leadership in language because the external environment doesn't do it for them.

---

**[ADVANCE → Stage 5: Strategic Options]**

And then there's one convergence — Strategic Options.

Every firm talks about optionality, choices, risk management at essentially the same rate: 7.3 to 8.9 per thousand words. This is the one IPM dimension where all four firms meet.

The reading: everyone weighs choices. They just choose differently. The options vocabulary is shared; what they do with it is not.

---

*[Pause. Let the six-lens picture settle.]*

Two shared themes — Long-term and Strategic Options. Two splits — Explore/Exploit and Customer/Shareholder. Two flips — Horizon Scanning and Strategic Leadership. That's the map. Now let's go company by company."

## SLIDE 04 — AMAZON: FOUNDER → OPERATOR → AI BUILDER

*[Three stages. The timeline chart is always visible.]*

"Amazon is the only firm in the corpus that gives us a CEO transition inside our window. Bezos founded the company, wrote letters from 1997 to 2021, and handed over to Andy Jassy in 2022. That transition is *visible in the data*.

---

**[ADVANCE → Stage 1: Bezos Era]**

The Bezos arc has two peaks. The first is 1997 — the founding letter, explore share at 0.54. Then it rises. By 2016 Bezos is at 0.94. That letter contains the phrase *'we are inventors'* — it is the most exploration-saturated letter in the entire corpus.

The pull quote from his *last* letter as CEO in 2021 captures the posture perfectly:

*'We're divinely discontented with customer experiences… Our job is both to listen to their feedback and to imagine what else is possible and invent on their behalf.'*

'Divinely discontented.' That's not operational language — that's a founding ideology dressed in CEO prose.

Notice also that across the Bezos years — founding letter, mid-career letters, pandemic year, farewell letter — the customer-obsession vocabulary stays rock-steady at around 13 per thousand words. That number doesn't move. It's a cultural constant.

---

**[ADVANCE → Stage 2: Jassy Era]**

Jassy's first full year — 2022 — is the sharpest explore-share drop in the Amazon timeline. From 0.80 in Bezos's farewell to 0.58 under Jassy.

And this isn't arbitrary. In 2022, Amazon announced 27,000 corporate layoffs, a warehouse rationalisation programme, and a capex pullback. The language *led* the 10-K disclosures. The vocabulary shifted before the annual report confirmed it.

Jassy's own words from that letter:

*'We reprioritized where to spend our resources, which ultimately led to the hard decision to eliminate 27,000 corporate roles. We'll continue to evaluate what we're seeing in our business and proceed adaptively.'*

'Evaluate' and 'adaptively' — that's the language of an operator managing a correction, not an inventor.

But then watch what happens. By 2024, Jassy's explore share climbs back to 0.75. Not Bezos-level, but a clear second phase. The driver is AI — Anthropic, Amazon Bedrock, Trainium custom silicon. The firm is exploring again, but within a bounded frame: AI is the single frontier, not the whole-company inventor posture.

The pattern across the full Amazon arc: founder explores broadly, operator exploits to fix efficiency, then re-explores around one specific frontier. Classic ambidexterity, playing out over 25 years of letters."

---

## SLIDE 05 — NVIDIA: THE EXPLORER BECAME THE EXPLOITER IN ONE YEAR

*[Three stages. The combined chart — explore-share line + datacenter revenue bars — is always visible.]*

"NVIDIA is the most dramatic story in the corpus. One firm. Same author — Jensen Huang has written every letter. But watch what happens to the vocabulary between 2021 and 2025.

---

**[ADVANCE → Stage 1: Pre-AI Boom (2017 lens)]**

In 2017, NVIDIA's datacenter revenue was $830 million. The letter opens with:

*'GPU computing has arrived. The tool that we built for the da Vincis and Einsteins of our time is the force behind today's most promising trends — from gaming and VR to AI and self-driving cars.'*

Da Vincis and Einsteins. That's the language of someone naming a new era before the rest of the world has caught up.

And across the 2017–2023 letters, that Horizon Scanning signature is extraordinary. NVIDIA scans at 27 per thousand words — five times the rate of anyone else in the corpus. Jensen Huang is not just writing a letter; he is continuously naming the next inflection. CUDA, deep learning, generative AI, AI factories — each letter re-names what's coming, ahead of schedule.

The 2021 letter is the explore peak: explore share at 0.864, exploit at 0.136. This is written *before* ChatGPT. Before the market has priced in what NVIDIA had already built. The vocabulary is pure frontier.

---

**[ADVANCE → Stage 2: Post-AI Boom (2025 lens)]**

Then comes 2025.

Revenue: $115 billion. Explore share: 0.348.

The flip is stark. For the first time, NVIDIA's letter is *exploit-leaning*. Same author, same firm. But the language has shifted from 'we are naming what's next' to 'we are building out the infrastructure of what we already announced.'

The 2025 letter:

*'A new industrial revolution is underway — transforming energy into artificial intelligence. The factory of this industrial revolution, an AI factory, is powered by computers created by NVIDIA.'*

'Factory.' 'Industrial revolution.' That's not exploration vocabulary — that's the language of scale-out, execution, delivery. The paradigm has been named. Now the job is to build it.

The revenue chart makes the arc legible. $0.83B to $115B. In vocabulary terms: the Strategic Options of 2017–2023 have matured into Agile Execution language by 2025. The textbook explore-to-exploit arc — compressed into eight years and traceable word by word.

NVIDIA is the cleanest case in the corpus for what explore-to-exploit actually looks like when it's working."

## SLIDE 06 — SHELL VS CHEVRON: EUROPEAN STEWARDSHIP / AMERICAN SHAREHOLDER

*[Three stages. The combined timeline — both firms on the same x-axis — is always visible.]*

"Now the energy side. Two firms that are, by every quantitative measure, similar: both exploit-dominant, both long-term oriented, both operating in the same commodity markets. But the *content* of their language is remarkably different.

---

**[ADVANCE → Stage 1: Firm Cards]**

**Shell** sits at 0.09 explore share across the corpus. That yellow line on the chart barely moves. It is the flattest trajectory in the dataset. Shell is not experimenting with its strategic vocabulary — it is disciplined, consistent, and almost entirely exploit-oriented.

What Shell does with its language is perform *stewardship*. The highest Strategic Leadership score in the corpus — 2.60 per thousand. The lower-carbon vocabulary appears from the 2014 letter onward and never leaves. It is institutional, not reactive.

The 2020 Shell letter captures the orientation:

*'Powering Progress combines our ambitions under four goals: generating shareholder value, achieving net-zero emissions, powering lives, and respecting nature.'*

Four goals. In that order. This is a European stakeholder model: shareholders matter, but so does the broader mandate.

**Chevron** is at 0.25 explore share — higher than Shell, though still firmly on the exploit side of the spectrum. And there's one moment of movement: 2021, when Chevron's explore share spikes to 0.375. That's the 'New Energies' launch — their first significant lower-carbon vocabulary investment. By 2024, that share is back down to 0.143.

The Chevron 2021 letter:

*'Our focus on "higher returns, lower carbon" is underpinned by operational excellence, cost discipline and capital discipline — and financial strength.'*

Note the syntax. 'Higher returns' comes first. 'Lower carbon' is the modifier. This is an American shareholder-primacy frame: the energy transition is legitimate insofar as it supports capital returns.

---

**[ADVANCE → Stage 2: Comparison Strip]**

The numbers that surface the cultural difference:

- Strategic Leadership per thousand words: Shell 2.60, Chevron 1.64. Shell *performs* leadership more.
- Agile Execution per thousand words: Shell 3.31, Chevron 6.43. Chevron narrates its operational machine more.
- Customer share: Shell 0.32, Chevron 0.27. Both are shareholder-voiced overall, but Shell edges toward stakeholders.

And here's the finding I think is methodologically interesting: both Shell and Chevron score high on the Purpose, Vision & Governance IPM theme — both around 10 per thousand words. Same theme, same score.

But the *content* of that theme is completely different. Shell's purpose vocabulary is 'energy progress,' 'lower carbon,' 'net zero.' Chevron's is 'return capital to shareholders,' 'capital discipline,' 'higher returns.' Same measurable theme — entirely different referents.

This is what the dictionary method is for. It's not just that we count governance vocabulary — we can see *which* governance vocabulary. And that's where the cultural difference lives."

## SLIDE 07 — THANK YOU

*[Let it breathe.]*

"To close, let me restate the core finding in one sentence:

**The way a firm talks about innovation is measurably consistent with the strategic mode it is actually operating in** — and the vocabulary differences between tech and energy are categorical, not gradual.

Long-term orientation is universal and tells us nothing. Explore-exploit orientation is the key differentiator, and it splits cleanly by industry. Customer versus shareholder voice reinforces that split. And within the industries, the *content* of shared themes — same keywords, different referents — surfaces the cultural differences that raw counts would miss.

Four companies. 28 letters. 61,000 words. And the strategy is in the prose — if you know how to read it.

Thank you.

## APPENDIX A — METHODOLOGY

*[Reference only — use if asked how the counting works.]*

"Three methodological choices, briefly:

**Stemming.** A single dictionary entry — say, 'innovate' — counts whether the letter writes *innovate*, *innovating*, *innovation*, or *innovative*. Without stemming, you can miss an entire vocabulary shift because the author changed inflection but kept the concept.

**Synonyms and excludes.** Each entry carries hand-picked synonyms — 'founder / founded / founding' all count together — and explicit exclusions to prevent false positives. The classic trap is Porter-stemmer collisions: *experiment* and *experience* share a stem, *productive* and *production* share a stem, *engineer* and *engine* share a stem. Every false positive was caught in iteration and added to the exclude list.

**Normalisation to per 1,000 words.** Letters range from 777 words (Shell 2015) to 5,251 words (Amazon 2022). Raw counts would just measure length. Normalisation turns the metric into *emphasis* — what fraction of available narrative each construct receives.

The dictionary is the methodology. The rest is counting."
